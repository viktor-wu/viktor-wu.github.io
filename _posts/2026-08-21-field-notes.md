It has now been a year since I started my PhD, and given the career pivot this is for me, I've been up to a lot of new things. I thought I'd start documenting the journey here, partly so I have somewhere to look back on it, and partly so that people in the rewilding community, academia, or elsewhere can get a sense of what I'm working on. 

After studies and work in physical geography, agriculture, and spatial statistics I took the leap into marine science as I was offered the exciting opportunity to bring together a lot of things I was interested in, but had never put together before: my background in satellite remote sensing, a growing interest in people and social science, and something completely new to me, bioacoustics. 

The most common piece of advice I've heard is to go easy on yourself in the first year, as it is all about finding your feet. A lot of people also say you never really find your feet, and I think I'm beginning to understand what they mean. It's been great fun being thrown into so many new environments, and I can confidently say that I haven't been bored once. But being new to many things has also been one of the biggest challenges of this PhD. Being surrounded by people with a background very different to mine, I often find myself feeling like a classic imposter, often struggling to follow discussions and jargon. I suppose this is also part of the nature of becoming an interdisciplinary researcher: you spend a lot of time stepping outside what you know and trying to understand how people from other disciplines think and work. Of course, being put in these situations has also meant learning a huge amount, very quickly, and personally I often find them more engaging and effective than learning from coursework.

My project has ended up focusing on seagrass (specifically seagrass restoration!). When learning about the many ecosystem services of seagrass, I was immediately very curious about its abilities to influence water clarity by reducing wave energy through its blades. Long before I knew anything about science, I had always judged a beach or body of water by its water clarity, and as a kid it was always my dream to swim in the most crystal-clear waters.

This effect is part of a seagrass-sediment-light feedback, as seagrass growth is highly dependent on light availability. From various experiments and measurements, this attenuating effect on waves has been demonstrated as water velocities are reduced. But the kid inside of me is naturally wondernig if this effect can be *seen* with our own eyes (or maybe from space!) as beautiful clear waters. So, what I've been doing this past year is studying this effect in UK waters and seagrass meadows, using satellite remote sensing. 

I've also had the opportunity to go out into the field at Loch Craignish (thank you to the lovely people at [Seawilding](https://www.seawilding.org/)!), and see seagrass restoration happening in person. I've been out to deploy HydroMoths, tiny hydrophones which will record acoustic data which I aim to use to detect fish sounds, and subsequently relate this to seagrass restoration and biodiversity changes. Fieldwork has been an absolute highlight of my PhD so far, especially when the sun is out!

Another really fun and very fruitful part of this year has been meeting so many great people. It's become clear to me that networking is a massive part of becoming a researcher, and there have been plenty of doors opened, and time saved, just by connecting with the right person. Most of all though, it's just been a lot of fun to meet people and make friends! Aside from a great department in Glasgow, I have found a brilliant community through the [NETGAIN DFA](https://netgain.wp.st-andrews.ac.uk/), where I really get to develop my interdisciplinary profile alongside my cohort, who I always look forward to meeting.

Other, more one-off events where I've met loads of great people include a summer school in bioacoustics (SeaBASS), conferences (IMA, MASTS and NETGAIN), and, where I currently am, in Icelandic and Greenlandic waters aboard the Maria S. Merian with the ALONGate 3 project.

If you're working on something related, or simply interested in any of the things I've written about here, I'd be very happy to hear from you. I'm always keen to connect with people working across rewilding, nature recovery, marine science, and beyond, so feel free to get in touch!
